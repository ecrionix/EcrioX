🎯 Learning Objectives

By conducting this interactive experiment, students and educators will achieve the following pedagogical goals:

  • Demonstrate why perceived frequency increases as a sound source approaches an observer (wavefront compression).
  • Demonstrate why perceived frequency drops as the sound source recedes from an observer (wavefront rarefaction).
  • Calculate observed frequency for moving source, moving observer, and combined motion scenarios.
  • Visualize the sonic barrier and Mach cone angle when source velocity equals or exceeds the speed of sound (v ≥ vs).

⚒ Mathematical Equations & Theoretical Formulas

The simulation models the following governing physical relationships:

f' = f_0 \left( \frac{v_s \pm v_{\text{obs}}}{v_s \mp v_{\text{source}}} \right), \quad \sin(\mu) = \frac{v_s}{v_{\text{source}}} \; [\text{Mach Cone}]

vs is the speed of sound in the medium (343 m/s in air at 20°C). Upper signs apply when moving toward one another (frequency increases); lower signs apply when moving away (frequency decreases).

📝 Step-by-Step Laboratory Procedure

  1. Step 1: Set the sound source frequency f0 = 440 Hz (Concert A) and speed of sound vs = 340 m/s.
  2. Step 2: Place the observer in the center and set the source speed to 68 m/s (approx 245 km/h, moving left to right).
  3. Step 3: Click 'Start Simulation' and observe the circular wavefronts compressing ahead of the source and expanding behind.
  4. Step 4: Verify that as the source approaches, the observed frequency rises to f' = 440 · [340 / (340 - 68)] = 440 · (340 / 272) = 550 Hz.
  5. Step 5: Observe the sudden pitch drop to f' = 440 · [340 / (340 + 68)] = 440 · (340 / 408) ≈ 367 Hz once the source passes the observer.
  6. Step 6: Increase source speed to 340 m/s (Mach 1.0) to observe wavefronts stacking into a shockwave.

📖 Scientific Explanation & Physics Theory

Named after Austrian physicist Christian Doppler, the Doppler effect is the change in frequency or wavelength of a wave in relation to an observer moving relative to the wave source. As a sound source moves through stationary air while emitting periodic pressure pulses, each successive wavefront is emitted from a position closer to the preceding wavefront in the direction of motion. The effective wavelength in front is shortened to λ' = (vs - v_source)/f0, resulting in a higher perceived pitch. Behind the source, wavefronts are spaced further apart (λ' = (vs + v_source)/f0), lowering the perceived pitch. When source speed matches or exceeds sound speed, wavefronts construct a constructive conical pressure wave (Mach cone) experienced on the ground as a sonic boom.

🌎 Real-World Engineering Applications

  • Police radar and lidar speed traps: Measuring frequency shifts of reflected electromagnetic radio waves to detect speeding vehicles.
  • Medical Doppler echocardiography: Measuring blood flow velocity and detecting cardiac valve leaks non-invasively.
  • Astrophysical redshift: Discovering the expansion of the universe and cosmic Big Bang through the redshift of light from receding distant galaxies.
  • Aviation weather radar: Detecting rotating mesocyclones and tornadoes in storm systems via radar frequency shifts.

💡 Worked Numerical Example

Practice Problem:

An ambulance emitting an 800 Hz siren travels at 34 m/s toward a stationary pedestrian on a sidewalk. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate: (a) Observed frequency as the ambulance approaches, (b) Observed frequency after the ambulance passes and recedes, (c) The percentage pitch drop heard by the pedestrian.

Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
1. Approaching source (v_source = 34 m/s, v_obs = 0):
f'_approach = f0 · [vs / (vs - v_source)] = 800 · [340 / (340 - 34)] = 800 · (340 / 306) ≈ 888.89 Hz.
2. Receding source (moving away):
f'_recede = f0 · [vs / (vs + v_source)] = 800 · [340 / (340 + 34)] = 800 · (340 / 374) ≈ 727.27 Hz.
3. Total frequency drop: Δf = 888.89 - 727.27 = 161.62 Hz.
4. Percentage drop: (161.62 / 888.89) · 100 ≈ 18.18% drop in perceived musical pitch (more than two whole semitones!).
