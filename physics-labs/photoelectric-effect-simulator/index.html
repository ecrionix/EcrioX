🎯 Learning Objectives

By conducting this interactive experiment, students and educators will achieve the following pedagogical goals:

  • Verify that photoelectron emission occurs only when incident light frequency meets or exceeds threshold frequency (f ≥ f0), regardless of light intensity.
  • Demonstrate that maximum kinetic energy (Kmax) scales linearly with photon frequency (hf) and is completely independent of light intensity.
  • Demonstrate that light intensity governs the number of emitted photoelectrons per second (photocurrent), but not individual electron energy.
  • Determine Planck's constant from the slope of the stopping potential versus frequency graph (slope = h/e).

⚒ Mathematical Equations & Theoretical Formulas

The simulation models the following governing physical relationships:

E = h f = \frac{h c}{\lambda}, \quad K_{\max} = h f - \phi = e V_0, \quad f_0 = \frac{\phi}{h}, \quad \lambda_0 = \frac{h c}{\phi}

Planck's constant h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s = 4.136 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV·s; Electron charge e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; Work function φ is the minimum energy required to liberate an electron from the metal surface.

📝 Step-by-Step Laboratory Procedure

  1. Step 1: Select Cesium as the target metal (Work function φ = 2.14 eV, threshold wavelength λ0 = 579 nm).
  2. Step 2: Set light wavelength to 700 nm (Red light, photon energy E = 1.77 eV) and set intensity to 100%.
  3. Step 3: Observe that ZERO electrons are emitted despite high intensity because E < φ (classic quantum proof).
  4. Step 4: Decrease wavelength to 400 nm (Violet light, photon energy E = 3.10 eV). Observe immediate electron emission with kinetic energy Kmax = 3.10 - 2.14 = 0.96 eV.
  5. Step 5: Apply a reverse stopping potential (retarding voltage) of -0.96 V and observe photoelectrons turning back right before the collector plate, reducing photocurrent to zero.

📖 Scientific Explanation & Physics Theory

Classical wave theory predicted that incident light waves would continuously transfer energy to electrons over time, meaning high-intensity red light should eventually eject electrons after a delay. In 1905, Albert Einstein resolved this crisis by proposing that light propagates as localized packets of quantized energy called photons, each carrying energy E = hf. An electron absorbs a single photon in an all-or-nothing interaction. If photon energy hf is less than the metal's work function φ, the electron cannot escape the surface potential barrier. If hf exceeds φ, the excess energy manifests instantaneously as electron kinetic energy: Kmax = hf - φ. Increasing light intensity merely increases photon arrival rate and photocurrent without changing individual photon energy.

🌎 Real-World Engineering Applications

  • Photomultiplier tubes (PMTs): Detecting single photons in high-energy physics, astrophysics, and medical scintillation scanners.
  • Solar photovoltaic panels: Converting incident sunlight photons directly into electrical current in semiconductor p-n junctions.
  • Night vision devices: Image intensifier tubes converting infrared and low-level visible photons into accelerated electron cascades.
  • Spectrophotometers: Measuring chemical concentration in analytical chemistry through light absorption and photodiode current.

💡 Worked Numerical Example

Practice Problem:

Ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 254 nm is incident on a clean zinc photocathode (work function φ = 4.31 eV). Given h = 4.1357 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV·s and c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s, calculate: (a) Photon energy in eV, (b) Threshold wavelength of zinc, (c) Maximum kinetic energy of emitted photoelectrons, (d) Stopping potential required to extinguish photocurrent.

Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
1. Photon Energy: E = hc / λ = (1240 eV·nm) / (254 nm) ≈ 4.882 eV.
2. Threshold Wavelength: λ0 = hc / φ = (1240 eV·nm) / (4.31 eV) ≈ 287.7 nm.
- Because incident λ (254 nm) < λ0 (287.7 nm), photon energy exceeds work function and photoelectrons ARE emitted!
3. Maximum kinetic energy: Kmax = E - φ = 4.882 - 4.31 = 0.572 eV.
4. Stopping potential: Since Kmax = e · V0, the stopping potential is exactly V0 = 0.572 Volts.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Wave theory predicted that electron kinetic energy should increase with wave amplitude (intensity), that any frequency of light should cause emission if shining long enough, and that there would be a measurable time delay for energy accumulation. Experiments proved emission is instantaneous, requires a threshold frequency, and kinetic energy depends only on frequency.
Doubling intensity doubles the number of photons hitting the metal per second. Consequently, exactly twice as many photoelectrons are emitted per second, doubling the measured photocurrent. However, the maximum kinetic energy and stopping potential of each electron remain completely unchanged.
Stopping potential (V0) is the negative retarding voltage applied between the anode and cathode that is just sufficient to stop the fastest emitted electrons (those with Kmax) from reaching the collector plate, reducing photocurrent to zero: e·V0 = Kmax.
