🎯 Learning Objectives

By conducting this interactive experiment, students and educators will achieve the following pedagogical goals:

  • Verify the Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum across elastic and inelastic collision scenarios.
  • Distinguish between elastic collisions (kinetic energy conserved) and inelastic collisions (kinetic energy converted to thermal energy and deformation).
  • Calculate final velocities using restitution coefficient e and relative approach/separation speeds.
  • Observe center-of-mass velocity constancy before, during, and after impact.

⚒ Mathematical Equations & Theoretical Formulas

The simulation models the following governing physical relationships:

m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2, \quad e = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{u_1 - u_2}

Linear momentum is conserved in all isolated collisions. Kinetic energy is conserved only when coefficient of restitution e = 1 (elastic collision). When e = 0, objects stick together.

📝 Step-by-Step Laboratory Procedure

  1. Step 1: Configure Cart 1 (m1 = 2 kg, u1 = 4 m/s) and Cart 2 (m2 = 2 kg, u2 = 0 m/s).
  2. Step 2: Set the restitution mode to 'Elastic (e = 1.0)' and click 'Start Collision'.
  3. Step 3: Observe that Cart 1 comes to a complete standstill while Cart 2 shoots forward with 4 m/s (complete velocity transfer between identical masses).
  4. Step 4: Repeat the experiment with 'Inelastic (e = 0.0)' mode to verify that both carts stick together and move at half initial velocity (2 m/s), confirming 50% kinetic energy dissipation.

📖 Scientific Explanation & Physics Theory

According to Newton's Third Law, when two bodies collide, Cart 1 exerts force F12 on Cart 2, and Cart 2 exerts equal and opposite force F21 = -F12 on Cart 1 over the contact duration Δt. Because impulses are equal and opposite (J1 = -J2), the total linear momentum of the system remains strictly invariant (P_initial = P_final). In perfectly elastic collisions (e = 1), microscopic repulsive electrostatic forces act like ideal springs with zero mechanical energy loss. In inelastic collisions (0 ≤ e < 1), work is performed in permanently deforming materials, generating acoustic sound waves and heat, resulting in a net decrease in macroscopic kinetic energy.

🌎 Real-World Engineering Applications

  • Automotive crash engineering: Designing crumple zones that deform inelastically to absorb collision kinetic energy and protect cabin occupants.
  • Nuclear physics: Moderating fast neutrons in nuclear reactors using elastic collisions with light water or graphite nuclei.
  • Billiards and curling: Precision angle and velocity transfers between identical colliding spheres.
  • Space probe orbital gravity assists: Treating spacecraft-planet gravitational interactions as hyperbolic elastic scattering collisions.

💡 Worked Numerical Example

Practice Problem:

A 3.0 kg cart traveling at +6.0 m/s collides head-on with a stationary 2.0 kg cart. Calculate final velocities if: (a) The collision is perfectly elastic (e = 1.0), (b) The collision is completely inelastic (e = 0.0).

Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
1. Elastic collision (e = 1.0):
v1 = [(m1 - m2)/(m1 + m2)]u1 = [(3 - 2)/(3 + 2)] · 6 = (1/5) · 6 = +1.2 m/s.
v2 = [2m1 / (m1 + m2)]u1 = [2 · 3 / (3 + 2)] · 6 = (6/5) · 6 = +7.2 m/s.
Check momentum: (3 · 6) + 0 = 18 kg·m/s. Final: (3 · 1.2) + (2 · 7.2) = 3.6 + 14.4 = 18.0 kg·m/s.
2. Inelastic collision (e = 0.0):
v = (m1 u1 + m2 u2) / (m1 + m2) = (3 · 6 + 0) / (3 + 2) = 18 / 5 = +3.6 m/s.
Kinetic energy lost: KE_initial = ½(3)(6²) = 54 J; KE_final = ½(5)(3.6²) = 32.4 J (loss of 21.6 J or 40%).

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Never in an isolated system. As long as no external net force acts on the colliding bodies (such as track friction or external cables), total linear momentum is strictly conserved across all physical collisions in the universe.
The missing mechanical kinetic energy is converted into microscopic internal thermal energy (heating up the bodies), structural plastic deformation (bending metal), and acoustic vibrational energy (the sound of the crash).
The maximum kinetic energy loss occurs in a perfectly inelastic collision (e = 0) where the bodies stick together. However, momentum conservation requires the combined mass to retain center-of-mass kinetic energy, so total kinetic energy cannot drop to zero unless total initial momentum was zero.
