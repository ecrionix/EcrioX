🎯 Learning Objectives

By conducting this interactive experiment, students and educators will achieve the following pedagogical goals:

  • Verify that the period of a simple pendulum is directly proportional to the square root of its length (T ∝ √L).
  • Demonstrate that oscillation period is independent of bob mass and initial release amplitude for small angles.
  • Determine local acceleration due to gravity (g) using slope analysis of T² versus L.
  • Observe continuous conservation of mechanical energy between potential energy (apex) and kinetic energy (equilibrium).

⚒ Mathematical Equations & Theoretical Formulas

The simulation models the following governing physical relationships:

T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}, \quad f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}, \quad E = m g L (1 - \cos\theta) + \frac{1}{2}m v^2

For small angular amplitudes (θ < 15°), the restoring torque is proportional to displacement, yielding simple harmonic motion with period independent of bob mass.

📝 Step-by-Step Laboratory Procedure

  1. Step 1: Set the string length L to 1.0 m, mass to 1.0 kg, and gravity to Earth standard (9.8 m/s²).
  2. Step 2: Displace the bob to an initial angle of 10° and click 'Release Pendulum'.
  3. Step 3: Observe the oscillation counter and stopwatch to record the time for 10 complete oscillations.
  4. Step 4: Calculate the measured period T = Total Time / 10 and compare with the theoretical value T = 2π√(1.0 / 9.8) ≈ 2.006 s.
  5. Step 5: Repeat for lengths of 0.25 m, 0.50 m, and 1.50 m to construct a T² versus L relationship.

📖 Scientific Explanation & Physics Theory

A simple pendulum consists of a point mass suspended by a light, inextensible string from a frictionless pivot. When displaced by an angle θ, the component of gravity perpendicular to the string acts as a restoring force: F_restoring = -mg sin(θ). For small angles (θ in radians), sin(θ) ≈ θ (within 1% error for θ < 14°), transforming the equation of motion into the standard simple harmonic oscillator equation: d²θ/dt² + (g/L)θ = 0. The angular frequency is ω = √(g/L), yielding the famous period formula T = 2π√(L/g). Notice that bob mass m does not appear in the equation: all masses fall with the same gravitational acceleration.

🌎 Real-World Engineering Applications

  • Horology: Christian Huygens invented the pendulum clock in 1656, which provided the world's most accurate timekeeping standard for nearly 300 years.
  • Gravimetry: Measuring microscopic variations in Earth's gravitational acceleration g to detect underground mineral and petroleum deposits.
  • Foucault Pendulum: Proving Earth's rotation on its axis through the apparent precession of the pendulum's oscillation plane.
  • Tuned mass dampers in skyscrapers: Massive pendulums installed inside towers (such as Taipei 101) counteracting earthquake and typhoon wind vibrations.

💡 Worked Numerical Example

Practice Problem:

A simple pendulum has a length of 1.25 meters on the surface of Mars (where g = 3.71 m/s²). Calculate: (a) Time period of oscillation, (b) Frequency of oscillation, (c) What length would produce a 2.00-second period on Earth (g = 9.80 m/s²)?

Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
1. Mars period: T = 2π√(L / g_Mars) = 2π√(1.25 / 3.71) = 2π√(0.3369) = 2π · 0.5804 ≈ 3.647 s.
2. Mars frequency: f = 1 / T = 1 / 3.647 ≈ 0.274 Hz.
3. Seconds pendulum on Earth (T = 2.0 s): Rearranging T² = 4π²L/g gives L = (g · T²) / (4π²) = (9.80 · 4.0) / (4 · 9.8696) = 39.2 / 39.478 ≈ 0.993 meters (approximately 1 meter).
