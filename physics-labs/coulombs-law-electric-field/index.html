🎯 Learning Objectives

By conducting this interactive experiment, students and educators will achieve the following pedagogical goals:

  • Verify Coulomb's Inverse-Square Law: Electrostatic force is inversely proportional to the square of separation distance (F ∝ 1/r²).
  • Apply the principle of superposition to calculate resultant electric field vectors from multiple point charges.
  • Visualize electric field lines originating on positive charges and terminating on negative charges.
  • Investigate electric dipoles and identify neutral points where net electric field vanishes.

⚒ Mathematical Equations & Theoretical Formulas

The simulation models the following governing physical relationships:

F = \frac{k_e |q_1 q_2|}{r^2}, \quad \vec{E} = \sum_{i} \frac{k_e q_i}{r_i^2}\hat{r}_i, \quad V = \sum_{i} \frac{k_e q_i}{r_i}

Electrostatic constant ke = 8.988 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². Like charges repel and opposite charges attract. Electric field points away from positive charges and toward negative charges.

📝 Step-by-Step Laboratory Procedure

📖 Scientific Explanation & Physics Theory

Charles-Augustin de Coulomb formulated in 1785 that the electrostatic force between two stationary point charges is directly proportional to the product of their magnitudes and inversely proportional to the square of their separation distance. Michael Faraday introduced the concept of the electric field E: a vector field surrounding every charge such that any test charge q0 placed at that location experiences a force F = q0·E. Electric field lines represent the path a free positive test charge would accelerate along; line density is proportional to local field strength, and field lines never intersect because electric force at any point has a unique direction.

🌎 Real-World Engineering Applications

  • Electrostatic precipitators: Ionizing air and capturing industrial fly ash and smoke particles in power plant smokestacks.
  • Laser printing and photocopying: Charging photoreceptor drums to attract toner particles according to electrostatic charge patterns.
  • Semiconductor devices: Field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) using electric fields to gate electron flow through silicon channels.
  • Capacitive touchscreens: Sensing the change in local electric field when a conductive human finger approaches the screen grid.

💡 Worked Numerical Example

Practice Problem:

Two point charges q1 = +5.0 µC and q2 = -3.0 µC are separated by a distance of 0.30 meters in vacuum (ke = 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²). Calculate: (a) The electrostatic force between them, (b) Whether it is attractive or repulsive, (c) The electric field magnitude at the midpoint between the two charges.

Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
1. Coulomb Force: F = ke |q1 · q2| / r² = (8.99 × 10⁹ · 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ · 3.0 × 10⁻⁶) / (0.30)² = (0.13485) / 0.09 = 1.498 N.
2. Force nature: Because the charges have opposite signs, the force is ATTRACTIVE.
3. Midpoint electric field (r_mid = 0.15 m):
- Both E1 (pointing away from q1) and E2 (pointing toward q2) point in the SAME direction (toward q2)!
- E1 = ke |q1| / r_mid² = (8.99 × 10⁹ · 5.0 × 10⁻⁶) / (0.15)² = 44,950 / 0.0225 = 1,997,778 N/C.
- E2 = ke |q2| / r_mid² = (8.99 × 10⁹ · 3.0 × 10⁻⁶) / (0.15)² = 26,970 / 0.0225 = 1,198,667 N/C.
- Total field at midpoint: E_net = E1 + E2 = 1,997,778 + 1,198,667 ≈ 3.20 × 10⁶ N/C directed toward the negative charge.
