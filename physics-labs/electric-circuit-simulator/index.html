🎯 Learning Objectives

By conducting this interactive experiment, students and educators will achieve the following pedagogical goals:

  • Verify Ohm's Law: Current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to voltage and inversely proportional to resistance.
  • Compare equivalent resistance, current, and voltage distribution in series versus parallel combinations.
  • Calculate power dissipation and observe how filament brightness scales with electrical power (P = I²R).
  • Use virtual ammeters and voltmeters to measure live circuit parameters without disrupting operation.

⚒ Mathematical Equations & Theoretical Formulas

The simulation models the following governing physical relationships:

V = I \cdot R, \quad R_{\text{series}} = R_1 + R_2, \quad \frac{1}{R_{\text{parallel}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}, \quad P = V \cdot I = I^2 R

In series circuits, current is identical across components while voltages add. In parallel circuits, voltage is identical across branches while currents divide.

📝 Step-by-Step Laboratory Procedure

  1. Step 1: Select 'Series Circuit' mode and set DC battery voltage to 12 V.
  2. Step 2: Set Resistor 1 to 20 Ω and Resistor 2 (Bulb) to 10 Ω.
  3. Step 3: Close the circuit switch and read total current I = V / (R1 + R2) = 12 / 30 = 0.40 A.
  4. Step 4: Inspect the voltage across the bulb (V = 0.40 · 10 = 4.0 V) and note bulb brightness.
  5. Step 5: Switch to 'Parallel Circuit' mode with the same component values and observe that total current jumps to 1.80 A because branch voltage is now the full 12 V.

📖 Scientific Explanation & Physics Theory

Electric current is the continuous drift of charge carriers (electrons) driven by an electromotive force (potential difference V). Georg Simon Ohm established that for ohmic conductors at constant temperature, current I is proportional to potential difference: V = IR. In a series circuit, charge must flow through every component sequentially; thus total resistance equals the sum of individual resistances, and failure of any single component breaks the entire circuit. In a parallel circuit, each branch connects independently across the common voltage rails; adding parallel branches decreases total equivalent resistance and increases total current drawn from the power supply.

🌎 Real-World Engineering Applications

  • Household electrical wiring: All domestic appliances are connected in parallel so each receives full 230V/120V mains voltage and can operate independently.
  • Automotive electronics: Headlight circuits wired in parallel so if one bulb filament burns out, the opposite headlight stays illuminated.
  • Electronic sensors: Voltage divider networks using thermistors or photoresistors to convert environmental changes into measurable voltage signals.
  • Power grid transmission: Step-up and step-down transformer networks minimizing I²R transmission power line heating losses.

💡 Worked Numerical Example

Practice Problem:

A 24 V power supply is connected to two resistors: R1 = 60 Ω and R2 = 40 Ω. Calculate total current, individual voltage drops, and total power consumed if they are connected: (a) In series, (b) In parallel.

Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
1. Series Connection:
- R_eq = R1 + R2 = 60 + 40 = 100 Ω.
- Total Current: I = V / R_eq = 24 / 100 = 0.24 A.
- Voltage drops: V1 = I · R1 = 0.24 · 60 = 14.4 V; V2 = I · R2 = 0.24 · 40 = 9.6 V (14.4 + 9.6 = 24 V).
- Power: P = V · I = 24 · 0.24 = 5.76 Watts.
2. Parallel Connection:
- 1/R_eq = 1/60 + 1/40 = (2 + 3)/120 = 5/120 ⇒ R_eq = 24 Ω.
- Branch Currents: I1 = 24/60 = 0.40 A; I2 = 24/40 = 0.60 A.
- Total Current: I_total = I1 + I2 = 0.40 + 0.60 = 1.00 A.
- Power: P = V · I = 24 · 1.00 = 24.0 Watts (over 4 times more power than series!).

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

In parallel, every light bulb receives the full source voltage, and adding more bulbs does not reduce the voltage across existing bulbs. In series, total resistance increases with each additional bulb, causing circuit current to drop and dividing the source voltage into smaller fractions across each bulb.
An ammeter measures current flowing through a line; it has near-zero internal resistance and must be connected in series. A voltmeter measures electrical potential difference between two points; it has extremely high internal resistance to avoid drawing current and must be connected in parallel.
In an ideal circuit model, a closed switch has zero resistance (acting as an ideal wire), and an open switch has infinite resistance (air gap). Real switches possess negligible contact resistance on the order of milliohms.
