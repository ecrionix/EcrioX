Equilibrium Constant & Mass Action Law

K_c = \frac{[C]^c [D]^d}{[A]^a [B]^b}, \quad Q_c \gtrless K_c, \quad \Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K

At chemical equilibrium, forward and reverse reaction rates are identical. If Q < K, the system shifts forward; if Q > K, it shifts reverse to re-establish equilibrium.

Many chemical reactions are reversible: products collide to reform reactants. Dynamic equilibrium is established when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction, causing macroscopic properties (concentrations, color, pressure) to remain constant. Henri Le Chatelier stated that if a dynamic equilibrium is subjected to a disturbance (change in concentration, temperature, or pressure), the system shifts in the direction that counteracts the disturbance.

Experimental Objectives

  • Demonstrate that dynamic equilibrium is an active state where forward and reverse rates are equal.
  • Calculate equilibrium constants (K_c) from equilibrium concentrations.
  • Predict equilibrium shifts in response to concentration, pressure, and temperature perturbations.
  • Differentiate between reaction quotient (Q) and true thermodynamic equilibrium constant (K).

Step-by-Step Laboratory Procedure

  1. Step 1:

    Select a reversible reaction system: Haber Ammonia Synthesis or NO₂/N₂O₄ gas equilibrium.

  2. Step 2:

    Adjust initial reactant and product concentrations and set the chamber temperature.

  3. Step 3:

    Watch the concentration-time graph until concentrations reach stationary plateau values.

  4. Step 4:

    Apply a stress: Inject extra reactant, increase pressure, or heat the system to observe Le Chatelier restoration.

Worked Example & Step-by-Step Calculation

Problem Statement:

For the Haber reaction N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) at 400°C, the equilibrium concentrations are [N₂] = 0.60 M, [H₂] = 0.20 M, and [NH₃] = 0.50 M. (a) Calculate K_c. (b) If [NH₃] is suddenly increased to 1.50 M, predict the direction of the shift.

Mathematical Solution & Derivation:

1. Equilibrium expression: K_c = [NH₃]² / ([N₂] × [H₂]³). 2. Calculate K_c: K_c = (0.50)² / (0.60 × (0.20)³) = 0.25 / (0.60 × 0.008) = 0.25 / 0.0048 ≈ 52.08. 3. Perturbation: [NH₃] increases to 1.50 M. Calculate reaction quotient Q_c: Q_c = (1.50)² / (0.60 × 0.008) = 2.25 / 0.0048 = 468.75. 4. Compare Q_c and K_c: Since Q_c (468.75) > K_c (52.08), the system contains excess products. By Le Chatelier's Principle, the reaction shifts to the LEFT (reverse direction) to consume NH₃ and restore equilibrium.

Common Student Misconceptions & Scientific Reality

💡 Critical Conceptual Insight: A frequent misconception is that adding a catalyst shifts the equilibrium position. A catalyst speeds up both forward and reverse reaction rates by the exact same factor, allowing the system to reach equilibrium faster without altering the equilibrium constant K_c or final yields.

Frequently Asked Questions on Chemical Equilibrium & Le Chatelier's Principle Simulator

Chemical equilibrium is dynamic because chemical reactions do not stop. Molecules continue reacting in the forward and reverse directions at precisely equal rates, keeping macroscopic concentrations invariant.

Increasing pressure shifts the equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas to relieve pressure. If both sides have equal moles of gas, pressure changes have zero effect on equilibrium position.

Temperature changes the fundamental rate constants k_forward and k_reverse unequally based on their activation energies (Arrhenius equation). Concentration and pressure changes shift positions to restore the same Kc.
